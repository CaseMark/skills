# Reference Safety Information

How to draft the reference safety information (RSI) in an Investigator's Brochure and why it matters. Source status is in `primary-sources.md`.

## Why the List Matters

- ICH E6 says the RSI in the IB is the reference point for expedited reporting of suspected unexpected serious adverse reactions. It should include a list of adverse reactions with their frequency and nature, and that list is used to decide whether a suspected serious adverse reaction is expected.
- Under 21 CFR 312.32, an adverse event or suspected adverse reaction is "unexpected" if it is not listed in the investigator brochure, or not listed at the specificity or severity observed. Hepatic necrosis is unexpected if the brochure refers only to elevated hepatic enzymes or hepatitis. Events mentioned as occurring with a drug class, or anticipated from the pharmacology, are still unexpected unless the brochure says they have occurred with this product.
- The list therefore decides what the sponsor reports on a short clock. Precision matters in both directions: omissions cause unexpected reports; vague entries leave the reporting question open.

## What the List Contains

A stand-alone, clearly headed table, one row per adverse reaction:

| Adverse reaction (preferred term and specificity) | Frequency | Nature and severity | Basis | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|

- Use the specificity the data support. Do not group a specific event under a vaguer term.
- Frequency from human data only where human data exist, with the denominator and exposure.
- Nature: serious or non-serious, grade or severity, onset and reversibility where known.
- Source: the study or report, with locator.

## Basis Labels

Label every row with one of:

- `Observed in humans`: reported in a human study of this product, with the study cited.
- `Anticipated from nonclinical findings`: seen in nonclinical studies at or near expected exposure, and the study cited. Frequency is not applicable.
- `Class effect`: reported with related products; cite the published or regulatory source. Say plainly that it has not been observed with this product.

A class or anticipated effect in the IB does not make it an observed reaction of this product. Never move an entry up the list (for example, label an anticipated effect as observed) to widen what counts as expected.

## First-in-Human Brochures

- State that no human adverse reactions are known.
- List anticipated effects from nonclinical target organs and exposure comparisons, labeled `Anticipated from nonclinical findings`.
- How a sponsor frames the RSI when no human reaction exists is a judgment for the sponsor and regulatory affairs; log the choice and flag it for review `[VERIFY]`.

## Changing the List

- Changes to the list change what is expected. Give each change an edition and a date, and list it in the summary of changes.
- Nonclinical findings that suggest a significant risk in humans must be reported to the regulator under 21 CFR 312.32(c)(1)(iii), and ordinarily lead to a safety-related change in the IB (excluding routine updates). Flag any new nonclinical finding of that kind to the sponsor when updating.
- Important new safety information may have to reach investigators before the next revision (21 CFR 312.55(b)).

## Checks

- Every adverse reaction in the Effects in Humans safety tables appears in the list with matching frequency, or the log explains why not.
- Every target organ named in Nonclinical Toxicology as a human concern has an entry or a stated reason for omission.
- Each row has a basis label and a source.
- The list is identifiable as the RSI, so it can be pointed to from the protocol and safety plan.
