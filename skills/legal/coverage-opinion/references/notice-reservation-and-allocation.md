## 1. Notice conditions and late-notice prejudice

Verified text of CG 00 01 04 13, Section IV.2: the named insured must see that the insurer is notified "as soon as practicable" of an occurrence or offense which may result in a claim (2.a); if a claim is made or suit brought, the insured must record the specifics and notify the insurer as soon as practicable, and "see to it that we receive written notice of the claim or 'suit' as soon as practicable" (2.b); and must send copies of demands, summonses and legal papers, authorize records, and cooperate (2.c).

New York (N.Y. Ins. Law 3420, text read in full on 2026-10-30):
- A liability policy issued or delivered in New York must provide that failure to give timely notice does not invalidate a claim unless the failure prejudiced the insurer (3420(a)(5)). A claims-made policy may still require the claim to be made during the policy period, a renewal or an extended reporting period.
- Burden of proof on prejudice (3420(c)(2)(A)): on the insurer if notice was given within two years of the time required under the policy; on the insured, injured person or claimant if notice was given more than two years after that time.
- Irrebuttable presumption of prejudice (3420(c)(2)(B)) if, before notice, the insured's liability was determined by a court or binding arbitration, or the insured settled the claim.
- Prejudice means the late notice "materially impairs the ability of the insurer to investigate or defend the claim" (3420(c)(2)(C)).
- The policy must also say notice failure does not invalidate a claim if notice was not reasonably possible within the time and was given as soon as reasonably possible (3420(a)(4)).
- Section 8 of L. 2008, ch. 388 (the prejudice amendment), as quoted in N.Y. Insurance Department OGC Opinion 09-06-08, makes it take effect on the 180th day after it became law and apply to policies issued or delivered in New York on or after that date and to actions under them. Because it is a 2008 law, that day fell no later than June 29, 2009, so any policy issued or delivered in New York after mid-2009 is governed by the prejudice rule. The exact effective date (commonly reported as January 17, 2009) is `[VERIFY]`; for a policy issued in 2008 or 2009, confirm it.

Other states: whether the insurer must show prejudice for late notice under an occurrence policy, and who bears the burden, is `[VERIFY]` unless confirmed in the register. Analyze the notice defense both with and without a prejudice requirement, and never recommend a denial on late notice alone where the governing rule is unconfirmed.

Prejudice analysis, when required: identify what the insurer could not do because of the delay (inspect the scene, interview witnesses, preserve evidence, participate in early motions or mediation), with evidence; a delay alone is not prejudice.

## 2. Clocks table

Each timed element runs from its own trigger. Never assume one anchor for all of them.

| New York written disclaimer, death or bodily injury from an accident in New York, policy issued or delivered in New York | Insurer has sufficient knowledge of facts entitling it to disclaim, or knows it will disclaim | As soon as is reasonably possible; an unexcused 48-day delay is unreasonable as a matter of law | N.Y. For every clock in the opinion, show: trigger, evidence of the trigger date, period, deadline or days elapsed (with weekday), and status (met, missed, running). If the trigger date is unknown, compute from the earliest plausible date and list the fact as an open item.

Default when no statute applies: send the reservation or disclaimer as soon as the ground is identified and the facts supporting it are confirmed. Where Florida 627.426 or New York 3420(d)(2) applies, the statute's clock overrides this default.

## 3. Reservation-of-rights and disclaimer content

A reservation letter (or recommended letter) must:
1. Identify the policy, period, insured and suit.
2. Quote each provision relied on, with form number and edition.
3. State the facts or allegations that raise each ground. A general reservation of "all rights under the policy" does not identify a ground.
4. State that the insurer is defending (if it is) and what it reserves: the right to deny indemnity on the stated grounds, to withdraw if no potential remains, to seek a declaration, and to seek reimbursement where the governing law allows (see Buss in `references/defense-and-indemnity-rules.md`).
5. Address the insured's rights that follow from the reservation, including independent counsel where a reservation creates a conflict (section 5).
6. Go to every insured the statute requires (Florida: the named insured; New York 3420(d)(2): the insured and the injured person or other claimant).

A reservation is not a disclaimer. In New York a reservation-of-rights letter does not satisfy the 3420(d) disclaimer requirement (described in Zappone v. Home Ins. Co. as prior Court of Appeals law). Lack of coverage in the first place (no policy covering the vehicle, person or risk) does not require a disclaimer; a denial resting on an exclusion does (Zappone).

## 4. Waiver and estoppel

- Illinois: an insurer that believes a complaint potentially within coverage is not covered must defend under a reservation of rights or seek a declaratory judgment; if it does neither and is later found to have wrongfully denied coverage, it is estopped from raising policy defenses, including late notice. Estoppel does not apply where there was no duty to defend, for example where the policy and complaint clearly show no potential for coverage (Ehlco).
- New York: an untimely disclaimer under 3420(d)(2) loses the defense (Jetco); the statute does not create coverage the policy never provided (Zappone).
- Defending without reserving, or reserving late, can waive or estop defenses in other states; the scope and whether prejudice to the insured is required are `[VERIFY]`.
- Florida: acknowledging a claim, furnishing forms, investigating and negotiating are not by themselves a waiver (Fla. Stat. 627.426(1)); the reservation and the 627.426(2) steps still apply.

## 5. Who is the client, conflicts and independent counsel

| Role of the requester | Can this skill draft the coverage opinion? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Coverage counsel retained by the insurer | Yes, addressed to the insurer | Must not also be defense counsel for the insured in the same matter |
| Coverage counsel retained by the insured or an additional insured | Yes, addressed to the insured | Insured-side opinion; tender and response strategy |
| Defense counsel retained by the insurer to defend the insured | No coverage opinion for the insurer against the insured | Defense counsel's client is the insured, and in tripartite states the insurer as well (California: absent a conflict, a dual attorney-client relationship with insurer and insured, State Farm v. Federal Ins.; other states `[VERIFY]`). Either way, counsel may not advise the insurer on coverage against the insured or disclose coverage-relevant confidences (Cal. Civ. Code 2860(d); ABA Model Rule 1.8(f)(3) and Rule 1.6); route the insurer to separate coverage counsel |
| In-house claims professional asking for an internal analysis | Yes, as a draft for the insurer's coverage counsel | Mark privileged status as counsel decides |
| A firm representing both insurer and insured on coverage | No | Direct conflict; hard stop |
| Broker or agent | Summary only; no legal opinion | Route to counsel |

California: if the policy imposes a duty to defend and a conflict of interest arises that creates a duty to provide independent counsel, the insurer must provide it unless the insured waives in writing (Cal. Civ. Code 2860(a)). No conflict exists as to allegations for which the insurer denies coverage, but when the insurer reserves rights on an issue whose outcome can be controlled by counsel first retained by the insurer for the defense, a conflict may exist; no conflict arises solely from punitive damages allegations or a claim above limits (2860(b)). The insurer may require minimum qualifications and pays rates it actually pays in similar actions (2860(c)). Independent counsel must disclose information to the insurer except privileged materials relevant to coverage disputes (2860(d)). Other states' independent counsel rules are `[VERIFY]`.

ABA Model Rule 1.8(f): a lawyer paid by someone other than the client (the insurer) may accept that payment only with the client's informed consent, no interference with independent professional judgment, and confidentiality protected under Rule 1.6. The state's adopted version controls `[VERIFY]`.

## 6. Multiple policies, other insurance and allocation

Build a policy matrix: carrier, policy number, layer, period, form and edition, limits, other-insurance clause, notice and tender date, response.

Verified text of CG 00 01 04 13, Section IV.4:
- 4.a: the insurance is primary except when 4.b applies; if other insurance is also primary, the insurers share by the method in 4.c.
- 4.b(1): it is excess over specific categories only: other insurance that is fire, extended coverage, builder's risk, installation risk or similar coverage for "your work"; fire insurance for premises rented to the named insured; insurance for tenant liability for damage to rented premises; certain aircraft, auto or watercraft insurance; and any other primary insurance available to the named insured for premises, operations or products-completed operations "for which you have been added as an additional insured". When excess, it has no duty to defend if another insurer has a duty to defend (4.b(2)).
- 4.c: contribution by equal shares if all other insurance permits it; otherwise by limits, in the ratio of each insurer's limit to the total.

Rules:
- An other-insurance clause governs sharing among insurers. It does not reduce the duty owed to the insured: a primary insurer whose policy is triggered defends while it pursues contribution. A successive-period CGL policy issued to the same named insured is not, by itself, one of the 4.b(1) categories; whether other-insurance clauses apply at all between successive policy periods is `[VERIFY]` by state.
- Allocation across periods follows the policy language in New York: pro rata allocation was applied to language limiting coverage to damage "during the policy period" (Consolidated Edison v. Allstate), and all-sums allocation with vertical exhaustion applied where non-cumulation and prior-insurance provisions were inconsistent with pro rata allocation (Matter of Viking Pump). Other states' allocation rules (pro rata by time on risk, all sums, treatment of uninsured years) are `[VERIFY]`.
- Tender to every other potentially triggered insurer in writing and record the response. An insured's choice to tender to one carrier, or a "selective tender", has state-specific consequences `[VERIFY]`.
- Additional insured coverage: read the AI endorsement itself (who is added, for what operations, ongoing or completed operations, "caused in whole or in part" wording) and any primary and noncontributory wording; a certificate of insurance is not the endorsement.
- Excess and umbrella: identify the attachment point, follow-form terms, drop-down provisions and exhaustion requirements; notify each layer.
