## 1. Who is covered (scope map)

| Status | Statute | Who it reaches | Jurisdictional hook |
|---|---|---|---|
| Issuer | 15 U.S.C. 78dd-1 | Company with a class of securities registered under 15 U.S.C. 78l or required to file reports under 78o(d) (includes foreign issuers with exchange-traded American Depositary Receipts, Resource Guide); its officers, directors, employees, agents, and stockholders acting on its behalf | Use of the mails or any means or instrumentality of interstate commerce (a); nationality-based alternative jurisdiction for U.S.-organized issuers acting outside the U.S. (g) |
| Domestic concern | 15 U.S.C. 78dd-2 | U.S. citizens, nationals and residents; entities with principal place of business in the U.S. or organized under U.S. law (h)(1); their officers, directors, employees, agents, stockholders | Interstate commerce (a); nationality-based alternative jurisdiction (i) |
| Any other person | 15 U.S.C. 78dd-3 | Foreign nationals and foreign entities not covered above, and their agents | Acting "while in the territory of the United States" through the mails, interstate commerce, or "any other act" (a) |
| Issuer only | 15 U.S.C. 78m(b)(2), (b)(5) | Books and records and internal accounting controls; knowing circumvention or falsification by any person | Issuer status; applies whether or not any bribe is involved |
| Relevant commercial organisation | UK Bribery Act 2010 s.7(5) | Body incorporated or partnership formed in the UK, or any body or partnership, wherever formed, that carries on a business or part of a business in the UK | s.7 offence applies wherever the associated person's bribery occurs (s.12(5)) |

Scope rules:

- A U.S. parent's foreign subsidiary is not itself an issuer or domestic concern by reason of ownership. It is reached through 78dd-3 (acts in U.S. territory), through agency for the parent, or through the parent's books and records, which consolidate the subsidiary's.
- For an issuer holding 50 percent or less of the voting power of a firm, 78m(b)(6) requires only good-faith efforts to use its influence to cause the firm to devise and maintain internal accounting controls; an issuer that demonstrates good-faith efforts is conclusively presumed to comply.
- A private U.S. company is a domestic concern for anti-bribery purposes but is not subject to 78m(b)(2) unless it is an issuer. Do not tell a private company that the FCPA books-and-records provision applies to it; record-keeping duties then come from tax, contract, lender and other law, and from UK Bribery Act adequate procedures where s.7 applies.
- Under the MoJ guidance (paragraph 36), a UK listing alone, or a UK subsidiary alone, does not by itself make a parent a relevant commercial organisation; a demonstrable business presence in the UK does.

## 2. Anti-bribery elements

78dd-1(a), 78dd-2(a) and 78dd-3(a) share the elements:

1. A covered person, using the jurisdictional hook for that status;
2. corruptly;
3. in furtherance of an offer, payment, promise to pay, or authorization of payment of money, or an offer, gift, promise or authorization of anything of value;
4. to a foreign official, a foreign political party or party official, a candidate for foreign political office, or any person while knowing that all or part will be passed to one of them;
5. to influence an official act or decision, induce a breach of lawful duty, secure any improper advantage, or induce the use of influence with a foreign government or instrumentality;
6. to assist in obtaining or retaining business for or with, or directing business to, any person.

"Knowing" (78dd-1(f)(2)) is satisfied by awareness, a firm belief, or awareness of a high probability that the circumstance exists, unless the person actually believes it does not. Deliberately avoiding the facts about an intermediary is knowledge.

The business nexus is broad. United States v. Kay, 359 F.3d 738 (5th Cir. 2004), held that bribes to secure illegally reduced customs duties and taxes can fall within "obtaining or retaining business". Customs and tax payments are therefore not outside the statute because no contract is awarded.

The statute has no minimum value. The Resource Guide states that items of nominal value (cab fare, reasonable meals, promotional items) have not, without more, led to enforcement, and that enforcement against small payments has involved a systemic course of conduct.

## 3. Foreign official and instrumentality

"Foreign official" (78dd-1(f)(1)(A)) means any officer or employee of a foreign government or any department, agency or instrumentality, or of a public international organization, or any person acting in an official capacity for any of them. Rank does not matter.

Instrumentality test (United States v. Esquenazi, 752 F.3d 912 (11th Cir. 2014)): an entity controlled by the government of a foreign country that performs a function the controlling government treats as its own.

- Control factors: formal designation by the government; majority government interest; government power to hire and fire the entity's principals; whether profits go to the government fisc and whether the government funds losses; how long these indicia have existed.
- Function factors: monopoly over the function; government subsidy of its costs; services to the public at large; whether the public and the government perceive the function as governmental.

Drafting consequences:

- Majority ownership is one control factor, not the test. A minority-owned entity can be controlled through appointment rights or a golden share; a majority-owned entity still needs the function inquiry. Do not write "any government ownership makes employees foreign officials" or "under 50 percent is safe". Classify each counterparty on the factors and record the basis.
- Typical candidates to classify: state-owned enterprises (national oil, power, telecom, airlines), public hospitals and their physicians, public universities, sovereign wealth funds, central banks, customs and port authorities, state media.
- The Esquenazi factors were stated by the Eleventh Circuit; other circuits have approved similar jury instructions (Resource Guide). Treat the factor list as persuasive outside the Eleventh Circuit.

## 4. Facilitating payments, affirmative defenses, extortion

**Exception (78dd-1(b), 78dd-2(b), 78dd-3(b)).** The prohibition does not apply to a facilitating or expediting payment whose purpose is to expedite or secure the performance of a "routine governmental action".

- "Routine governmental action" (78dd-1(f)(3)) is an action ordinarily and commonly performed in obtaining permits or licenses to qualify to do business, processing papers such as visas and work orders, police protection, mail, scheduling inspections associated with contract performance or transit of goods, utilities, cargo loading and unloading, protecting perishables, and similar actions.
- It does not include any decision whether, or on what terms, to award or continue business, and the Resource Guide states it does not include acts within an official's discretion or misuse of office.
- The exception turns on purpose, not size, though a large payment suggests a non-routine purpose (Resource Guide).
- A payment to make an inspector overlook a deficiency, to clear goods that failed inspection, or to reduce duties is not a facilitating payment. DOJ's July 17, 2026 resolution with The Scoular Company involved payments of about $2,000 per train through customs brokers to Mexican officials so that shipments passed inspection despite impurities, invoiced back as "reinspection fees"; it was charged as conspiracy to violate the anti-bribery provisions.
- A payment within the exception must still be recorded accurately by an issuer (Resource Guide hypothetical) and may violate local law or the UK Bribery Act.

**Affirmative defenses (78dd-1(c)).** The defendant bears the burden.

1. Lawful under the written laws and regulations of the official's country. Custom, tolerance, or unwritten practice is not enough.
2. A reasonable and bona fide expenditure, such as travel and lodging, directly related to (A) the promotion, demonstration or explanation of products or services, or (B) the execution or performance of a contract with a foreign government or agency.

**Extortion and duress.** A payment made in response to a true extortionate demand under imminent threat of physical harm is not made with corrupt intent; mere economic coercion (pay to enter a market or win a contract) is not extortion (Resource Guide, quoting the legislative history).

## 5. Accounting provisions

Apply to issuers whether or not any bribe is involved.

- **Books and records (78m(b)(2)(A)).** Make and keep books, records and accounts that, in reasonable detail, accurately and fairly reflect transactions and dispositions of assets.
- **Internal accounting controls (78m(b)(2)(B)).** Devise and maintain controls sufficient to provide reasonable assurances that transactions are executed under management's authorization, recorded to permit GAAP financial statements and maintain accountability for assets, that access to assets is authorized, and that recorded accountability is compared with existing assets at reasonable intervals.
- "Reasonable detail" and "reasonable assurances" mean the level that would satisfy prudent officials in the conduct of their own affairs (78m(b)(7)).
- **No materiality threshold** (Resource Guide). A mis-described $2,000 payment is a books-and-records problem even if immaterial to the financial statements.
- **Criminal liability** only for knowingly circumventing or failing to implement controls, or knowingly falsifying a book, record or account (78m(b)(4)-(5)), punished through 78ff(a).
- Typical mischaracterizations: bribes booked as consulting, commissions, "market research", "logistics surcharges", "reinspection fees", per diems, marketing support, charitable donations, or miscellaneous expense; round-sum invoices; petty cash without receipts.

## 7. Limitation periods and other clocks

Each element runs from its own trigger. None of these was changed by Executive Order 14209 or the June 2025 guidelines.

| Element | Trigger event | Period | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Criminal anti-bribery charge | Commission of the offense (each payment, offer or authorization) | 5 years | 18 U.S.C. 3282(a) |
| Criminal accounting-provision charge (78ff(a)) | Commission of the offense | 6 years ("securities fraud offense") | 18 U.S.C. 3301(a)(2), (b) |
| Conspiracy (18 U.S.C. 371) | Last overt act in furtherance within the period | Reaches earlier payments if one overt act falls inside (Resource Guide) | Resource Guide, Statute of Limitations |
| Suspension to obtain foreign evidence | Official request to the foreign authority | Up to 3 years, by court order | 18 U.S.C. 3292 |
| SEC or DOJ civil penalty | Date the claim first accrued | 5 years (tolled while the offender is not found in the U.S.) | 28 U.S.C. 2462 |
| SEC disgorgement | Latest date of the violation | 5 years; 10 years where scienter must be established | 15 U.S.C. 78u(d)(8)(A) |
| SEC equitable remedies (injunction, bar) | Latest date of the violation | 10 years | 15 U.S.C. 78u(d)(8)(B) |
| DOJ opinion procedure response | DOJ's receipt of a complete request | 30 days | 78dd-1(e)(1); 78dd-2(f)(1) |
| M&A self-disclosure presumption of declination | Closing date of the acquisition | Disclose generally within 180 days; remediate generally within 1 year | Justice Manual 9-28.900 |
| CEP whistleblower exception | Company's receipt of the whistleblower's internal report | Self-report no later than 120 days | DOJ CEP (March 2026), Appendix B |

Worked example: a payment made on Tuesday, September 30, 2025 can be charged as an anti-bribery offense until Monday, September 30, 2030, before any tolling agreement, 3292 suspension or conspiracy extension.

## 8. Foreign Extortion Prevention Act (demand side)

18 U.S.C. 1352, enacted in its current form by Pub. L. 118-78 (July 30, 2024), which repealed the earlier version in section 5101 of the FY2024 NDAA (Pub. L. 118-31).

- Makes it a crime for a foreign official, or a person selected to be one, to corruptly demand, seek, receive, accept or agree to accept anything of value from an issuer, a domestic concern, or (while in U.S. territory) any person, in return for official action, breach of duty, improper advantage or influence, in connection with obtaining or retaining business.
- "Foreign official" here also includes senior foreign political figures as defined in 31 C.F.R. 1010.605.
- Penalty: fine up to $250,000 or 3 times the value of the thing demanded, prison up to 15 years; extraterritorial jurisdiction.
- It does not reach the payer's conduct that violates the FCPA (rule of construction, (b)(5)).
- Policy consequence: employees and intermediaries report every solicitation or demand promptly; the report is evidence for the company and for any FEPA referral. FEPA does not create a defense for the payer.

## 9. UK Bribery Act 2010

- **s.1** bribing another person; **s.2** being bribed; **s.6** bribing a foreign public official with intent to influence the official in that capacity and intent to obtain or retain business or an advantage in the conduct of business, unless the official is permitted or required by the applicable written law to be influenced (s.6(3)(b), (7)).
- **Foreign public official (s.6(5))** includes anyone exercising a public function for a public agency or public enterprise of a country outside the UK.
- **s.7 failure to prevent.** A relevant commercial organisation is guilty if a person associated with it bribes another person intending to obtain or retain business or an advantage for it. Defence: the organisation proves it had in place adequate procedures designed to prevent associated persons from bribing (s.7(2)).
- **Associated person (s.8).** Anyone who performs services for or on behalf of the organisation, in any capacity (employee, agent, subsidiary), judged on all the circumstances; employees are presumed to be associated.
- **Territorial reach (s.12).** s.7 is committed irrespective of where the acts take place; ss.1, 2 and 6 reach acts abroad by persons with a close connection to the UK.
- **Penalties (s.11).** Individuals under ss.1, 2 or 6: up to 10 years on indictment; s.7: an unlimited fine on indictment.
- **No facilitation-payment exception.** MoJ guidance paragraphs 44-45: small payments to facilitate routine government action may trigger s.6 or s.1, and therefore s.7; unlike U.S. law there is no exemption. Paragraph 48: the common-law defence of duress is very likely available where payments are made to protect against loss of life, limb or liberty.
- **Adequate procedures (s.9 guidance, MoJ 2011; gov.uk page last updated January 22, 2025).** Six principles: proportionate procedures; top-level commitment; risk assessment; due diligence; communication (including training); monitoring and review. Reasonable and proportionate bona fide hospitality is not targeted (paragraph 26).
- **SFO Guidance on Evaluating a Corporate Compliance Programme** (published November 26, 2025) explains how the SFO assesses programmes, including for the s.7 adequate procedures defence and the "reasonable procedures" defence to failure to prevent fraud under s.199 of the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023.

## 10. U.S. enforcement posture as of 2026-10-30

This section changes; the policy states it with the date checked and does not draft around it.

- **Executive Order 14209 (February 10, 2025; 90 FR 9587).** Directed the Attorney General, for 180 days (extendable by 180), to cease initiating new FCPA investigations absent an individual exception, review existing matters, and issue updated guidelines; later investigations must be governed by those guidelines and specifically authorized. It is an order on enforcement discretion. It does not amend the statute, and section 4(c) says it creates no enforceable right.
- **DOJ Guidelines for Investigations and Enforcement of the FCPA (June 9, 2025, Deputy Attorney General).** New FCPA investigations require authorization by the Assistant Attorney General for the Criminal Division or a more senior official. Factors: links to cartels and transnational criminal organizations; conduct that deprived specific U.S. companies of fair competition; national security (critical minerals, ports, infrastructure, defense); serious misconduct with strong indicia of corrupt intent tied to individuals (substantial payments, sophisticated concealment, fraud, obstruction), not routine business practices or de minimis, generally accepted courtesies; and whether a foreign authority will act. FEPA cases consider harm to U.S. entities. The memo states it creates no enforceable right. The DOJ FCPA Guidelines page (updated June 10, 2025) lists no later FCPA-specific guidelines.
- **DOJ Corporate Enforcement and Voluntary Self-Disclosure Policy (March 10, 2026).** Department-wide (except antitrust); supersedes component policies, including the Criminal Division's May 2025 CEP. Part I: declination for voluntary self-disclosure, full cooperation, timely and appropriate remediation and no aggravating circumstances, with disgorgement and restitution. Part II: NPA under three years, no monitor, 50-75 percent reduction off the low end of the Guidelines range for near-miss disclosures or aggravating factors. Part III: discretion, reduction no more than 50 percent. Remediation includes an effective compliance program and controls on personal and ephemeral messaging.
- **Evaluation of Corporate Compliance Programs.** The latest version is September 2024 (the Criminal Division page, updated October 6, 2026, still lists it). Three questions: is the program well designed; is it adequately resourced and empowered; does it work in practice. It directs prosecutors to probe for a "paper program" and covers risk assessment (including gifts, travel, third parties, charitable and political donations), policies, training, reporting and investigations, third-party management, M&A, incentives and discipline, and continuous improvement and testing.
- **Enforcement continues.** DOJ press release of July 17, 2026 (Scoular DPA, conspiracy to violate the anti-bribery provisions, conduct 2013-2019). Secondary sources report a March 2026 CEP declination, an October 2025 corporate indictment, and limited SEC FCPA activity; those reports are not relied on for any rule.
- **Drafting rule.** The policy prohibits what the statute prohibits. A future administration, the SEC, foreign authorities, and private litigants are not bound by the current priorities, and the limitation periods in section 7 outlast any review period.

## 11. Local law

- The written-law defense needs the written law itself; a local lawyer's opinion that a practice is "customary" does not satisfy it.
- Local law can be stricter (gift caps for public employees, bans on hospitality for procurement staff, domestic anti-bribery codes, corporate liability statutes). Apply the stricter rule and cite it, or mark it `[VERIFY]`.
- Local law can conflict with program design: data-protection limits on hotline scope and cross-border transfer of investigation data, works-council consultation, labor-law limits on discipline, and blocking statutes. Name the conflict and route it to local counsel; never resolve it by dropping the control.
