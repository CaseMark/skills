## 1. AKS elements and intent

- **Conduct** (1320a-7b(b)(1)-(2)): knowingly and willfully offering, paying, soliciting or receiving any remuneration, directly or indirectly, overtly or covertly, in cash or in kind, to induce or in return for referring an individual, or purchasing, ordering, arranging for or recommending any item or service payable in whole or part by a Federal health care program.
- **Who**: anyone. The AKS is not limited to physicians, to DHS, or to Medicare. Marketers, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, hospitals, labs, home health agencies and manufacturers are all covered.
- **Federal health care program** (1320a-7b(f)): any plan funded directly, in whole or in part, by the United States (other than the FEHB program under 5 U.S.C. chapter 89) and any State health care program, so Medicare, Medicaid (including managed care), TRICARE and others. Commercial-only business is outside the AKS but not outside state law or EKRA (section 4).
- **Intent**: "knowingly and willfully"; no actual knowledge of the AKS or specific intent to violate it is required (1320a-7b(h), added by the ACA in 2010).
- **One-purpose test**: a payment violates the statute if one purpose was to induce referrals, even if it also pays for real services. Read and confirmed for the Third Circuit (United States v. Greber, 760 F.2d 68 (3d Cir. 1985)), the Ninth (United States v. Kats, 871 F.2d 105 (9th Cir. 1989)), the Tenth (United States v. McClatchey, 217 F.3d 823 (10th Cir. 2000)), and the Seventh (United States v. Borrasi, 639 F.3d 774 (7th Cir. 2011), which also follows the Fifth Circuit in United States v. Davis, 132 F.3d 1092 (5th Cir. 1998)). Other circuits: `[VERIFY]`; analyze under the governing circuit's standard or, if unconfirmed, under both a one-purpose and a primary-purpose reading.
- **Intent evidence to collect**: emails, board and committee minutes, pro formas or dashboards that track a physician's referrals, timing of the arrangement against referral shifts, payments without time records, services not needed, rates above market, selection of physicians by referral volume. One document showing a referral purpose can carry the analysis even where the price is FMV.

## 2. Safe harbors and how they differ from Stark exceptions

Safe harbors are voluntary: meeting every element protects the arrangement; missing one does not make it illegal, but the arrangement is then judged on its facts and intent. Stark exceptions are mandatory. Meeting a Stark exception does not satisfy a safe harbor, and the reverse is also true. One link runs the other way: the Stark FMV compensation exception requires that the arrangement "does not violate the anti-kickback statute" (411.357(l)(5)), so an AKS violation also defeats that Stark exception.

| Arrangement | Safe harbor | Elements that differ from the Stark exception |
|---|---|---|
| Space lease | 1001.952(b) | Lease covers all premises leased between the parties; for part-time use, the exact schedule, length and rent of each interval; aggregate rental set in advance (Stark accepts a set per-unit rate); term at least 1 year; FMV excludes proximity to referral sources |
| Equipment lease | 1001.952(c) | Same structure; aggregate rental set in advance, so a per-click lease cannot meet it |
| Personal services and management | 1001.952(d)(1) | Signed writing covering all services the agent provides; term at least 1 year; compensation methodology set in advance, FMV, not taking into account the volume or value of federal program referrals or business; aggregate services not beyond what is reasonably necessary for a commercially reasonable business purpose. Outcomes-based payments: 1001.952(d)(2), not available to manufacturers, PBMs, labs and others listed in (d)(3) |
| Employment | 1001.952(i); statutory exception 1320a-7b(b)(3)(B) | Any amount paid to a bona fide employee (IRC 3121(d)(2) definition) for employment in furnishing covered items or services; not available to independent contractors; EKRA's employee exception is narrower (section 4) |
| Practitioner recruitment | 1001.952(n) | Read the full text; health professional shortage area conditions |
| Value-based | 1001.952(ee) care coordination (in-kind only; recipient pays at least 15 percent of the offeror's cost or the FMV of the in-kind remuneration; commercially reasonable), (ff) substantial downside financial risk, (gg) full financial risk | Pharmaceutical manufacturers, PBMs, laboratory companies, compounding pharmacies, device and DMEPOS suppliers and others are excluded from some or all; Stark value-based exceptions (411.357(aa)) are broader and have no FMV element |

Record element by element: Met, Not met, Unknown, with source. Where no safe harbor is met, state the facts-and-circumstances assessment and the label from `references/output-template.md`.

## 3. FCA link and penalties

- A claim that "includes items or services resulting from" an AKS violation is a false or fraudulent claim under the FCA (1320a-7b(g), added 2010).
- Causation under "resulting from" is circuit-variable. But-for causation: First Circuit (United States v. Regeneron Pharms., Inc., 128 F.4th 324 (1st Cir. 2025)), Sixth (United States ex rel. Martin v. Hathaway, 63 F.4th 1043 (6th Cir. 2023)) and Eighth (United States ex rel. Cairns v. D.S. Med. LLC, 42 F.4th 828 (8th Cir. 2022)), as described in Regeneron. Third Circuit: a "link" is required, less than but-for, with at least one claim for care provided in violation of the AKS (United States ex rel. Greenfield v. Medco Health Sols., Inc., 880 F.3d 89 (3d Cir. 2018)). Other circuits: `[VERIFY]`. Causation affects FCA damages, not whether the AKS was violated.
- Criminal: felony, fine up to $100,000 and imprisonment up to 10 years per violation (raised from $25,000 and 5 years by the Bipartisan Budget Act of 2018).
- Civil money penalty: 42 U.S.C. 1320a-7a(a)(7), up to $100,000 per act plus up to three times the total remuneration, without regard to whether part of it had a lawful purpose. The 2025 inflation-adjusted maximum per act in 45 C.F.R. 102.3 is $127,973; whether a 2026 adjustment has issued is `[VERIFY]`. Exclusion is also available.
- Hand FCA damages, qui tam and settlement exposure to conducting-false-claims-analysis.

## 4. State and other all-payor laws

Always screen. State laws often reach commercial patients, non-physician practitioners, and conduct that the federal laws do not.

| Law | Reach | Note |
|---|---|---|
| EKRA, 18 U.S.C. 220 | Referrals to recovery homes, clinical treatment facilities and laboratories, for services covered by any "health care benefit program" (public or private) | Up to $200,000 and 10 years per occurrence; employer and contractor payment exception only if pay does not vary by referrals, tests performed or amounts billed (220(b)(2)), narrower than the AKS employee exception |
| Florida Patient Brokering Act, Fla. Stat. 817.505 | Any person, including providers and facilities, paying or receiving anything to induce or in return for referral of patients or patronage, any payer | Felony; degree and fine rise with patient count (10-19 and 20 or more); (3)(a) excludes payment practices "not prohibited by 42 U.S.C. s. 1320a-7b(b)"; how that exception applies to commercial-only patients is `[VERIFY]` |
| Fla. Stat. 400.474(6)(e), (h), (i) (home health agencies; 2025 Florida Statutes) | A home health agency may not pay a case manager, discharge planner, facility staff member or vendor involved in discharge planning at a licensed facility that refers to it ((e)); may pay a physician only under a medical director contract that is written and signed, FMV hourly rate supported by invoices of work, dates and duration, term at least 1 year, with no rate increase during the term ((h)); may not pay a physician in violation of (g) or (h), or a physician's office staff or immediate family member, if it received a referral from that physician or office staff in the preceding 12 months ((i)) | License denial, revocation or suspension and a $5,000 fine; (e) and (i) do not reach practices permitted by the AKS, its safe harbors, or Stark and its regulations; whether 2026 amendments changed the text is `[VERIFY]` |

## 5. Advisory opinions and prior opinions

- **OIG advisory opinions** address AKS and related CMP questions (42 U.S.C. 1320a-7d(b)(2)). They are binding only as to the Secretary and the requesting parties (1320a-7d(b)(4)(A)); "no individual or entity other than the requestor(s) may rely on an advisory opinion" (42 C.F.R. 1008.53). They may not address whether FMV was paid or whether someone is a bona fide employee (1320a-7d(b)(3)). They do not decide Stark questions.
- **CMS Stark advisory opinions** (1395nn(g)(6); 42 C.F.R. 411.370-411.389): binding on the Secretary and protect the requestors and parties to the specific arrangement (411.387(a)); CMS will not pursue 1877(g) sanctions against a party to an arrangement that CMS determines is indistinguishable in all material aspects (411.387(b)); others may use an opinion only as non-binding guidance (411.387(c)).
- A copied structure differs in material facts more often than drafters admit: list every difference between the opinion's facts and the client's, and analyze each.
- Prior counsel opinions: read the facts the opinion assumed; an opinion built on incomplete facts, or one of several opinions where the client followed only the favorable one, does not support good faith (Drakeford v. Tuomey, 792 F.3d 364 (4th Cir. 2015)).

## 6. Disclosure paths and the 60-day rule

| Conduct | Path | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Stark only, no potential AKS liability for the same arrangement | CMS Voluntary Self-Referral Disclosure Protocol (SRDP), using the current OMB-approved CMS-10328 forms (revised September 2026); financial analysis limited to the 6-year lookback of 42 C.F.R. 401.305(f) | ACA 6409; CMS SRDP page; SRDP protocol (2025 OMB submission copy); OIG SDP (2026), sections 2.C and 7 |
| AKS only, or AKS and Stark together | OIG Health Care Fraud Self-Disclosure Protocol (SDP), updated October 15, 2026; the submission must acknowledge potential AKS (and, if applicable, Stark) violations | OIG SDP (2026), sections 3.D and 7 |
| Never | Both protocols for the same arrangement | OIG SDP (2026), section 7 |
| Overpayment without a disclosure | Contractor refund process | 42 C.F.R. 401.305(d)(1) |

- OIG SDP terms: minimum settlement $100,000 for kickback-related matters and $20,000 for others; general minimum multiplier of 1.5 times single damages; the discloser agrees to waive statute of limitations defenses within the CMP 6-year period; the SDP is not available for disclosing federal crimes, which should be disclosed to DOJ (section 2.C), but criminal conduct disclosed through the SDP is referred to DOJ, and a company's voluntary self-disclosure to DOJ under its Corporate Enforcement and Voluntary Self-Disclosure Policy for conduct involving a Federal health care program receives the benefits of the SDP (section 6). Clocks: findings and damages estimate within 90 days of the initial submission if not complete at submission; an improper kickback arrangement terminated within 90 days of submission (section 2.E).
- 60-day rule: report and return by the later of 60 days after identification or the cost report due date (42 U.S.C. 1320a-7k(d)); an overpayment is identified when the person knowingly receives or retains it (FCA "knowingly" standard) (42 C.F.R. 401.305(a)(2)); the deadline is suspended during a timely, good-faith investigation for related overpayments until the earlier of its completion or 180 days after the initial identification (401.305(b)(3)), and while an acknowledged OIG SDP or CMS SRDP submission is pending (401.305(b)(2)); 6-year lookback (401.305(f)). Retaining an overpayment past the deadline is an FCA "obligation" (1320a-7k(d)(3)).
- The choice among disclosure, refund and no action is a policy election for the client and counsel; the opinion states the options, the facts each depends on, and the recommendation.
