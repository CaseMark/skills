## 2. Hospital NPG.14.05.01 elements of performance

- **EP 1**: obtain information on the medications the patient is currently taking at admission or an outpatient visit, documented in a list or other useful format. Note 1: scheduled and as-needed medications. Note 2: a good-faith effort to obtain the information from the patient and/or other sources meets the intent. Documentation is required.
- **EP 2**: the hospital defines the medication information (for example name, dose, route, frequency, purpose) collected in non-24-hour settings such as the emergency department, primary care, outpatient radiology, ambulatory surgery and diagnostic settings.
- **EP 3**: compare the medication information the patient brought with the medications ordered by the hospital to identify and resolve discrepancies. Discrepancies include omissions, duplications, contraindications, unclear information and changes. A qualified individual, identified by the hospital, does the comparison.
- **EP 4**: give the patient (or family, caregiver or support person) written information on the medications to take at discharge or at the end of an outpatient encounter (for example name, dose, route, frequency, purpose). Documentation is required.
- **EP 5**: explain the importance of managing medication information at discharge or the end of an outpatient encounter (give the list to the primary care provider, update it on changes including OTC products, carry it).

Joint Commission FAQ (February 17, 2026) for non-24-hour settings: the requirements apply where medication may be administered or the regimen changed; the organization defines its process; if only a short-term medication is added, written information on that medication suffices; if nothing changed, no list is expected unless the organization requires one.

Related hospital NPGs: NPG.14.04.01 (anticoagulant therapy; EP 2 perioperative management of oral anticoagulants, including timing for stopping and restarting) and NPG.06.01.01 EP 6 (practitioner and pharmacist access to PDMP databases, whether querying is voluntary or mandated by the state).

## 3. Other Joint Commission programs (NPSG.03.06.01)

The 2026 nursing care center and home care NPSG.03.06.01 texts follow the same pattern: obtain current medication information on admission or acceptance and update it when medications change (for example after a hospital stay), compare it with the organization's orders to identify and resolve discrepancies, give written information, and explain its importance. Joint Commission FAQ (October 2, 2025, home care): even a limited-scope service such as infusion-only must reconcile; for drugs the patient takes independently the list may hold names only, but drugs the organization supplies or administers need name, dose, route, frequency, diluent if any, and duration.

## 4. WHO High 5s medication reconciliation SOP

Version 3, September 2014, posted by WHO February 23, 2021.

1. BPMH: all prescribed and non-prescribed medications (prescription, OTC, complementary or herbal, recreational, PRN), by systematic interview with patient and/or family where possible.
2. Verify with more than one source (community pharmacists, physicians, home care providers, medication containers, patient lists, government databases, previous records). The BPMH records name (generic and brand), dose, frequency and route and reflects actual use.
3. Reconcile at admission, proactive (BPMH before admission orders) or retroactive (orders first), with the goal of reconciliation within 24 hours of admission. Discrepancies are undocumented intentional or unintentional.
4. Communicate the Best Possible Medication Discharge Plan to the patient, community pharmacy, primary care physician or next facility.

The SOP states that reconciliation at transfer and discharge was not evaluated in the project, and Phase 1 targeted patients 65 or older admitted through the emergency department. Treat its 24-hour goal as a recommendation, not a regulatory deadline.

## 5. AHRQ MATCH toolkit

Gleason KM et al., AHRQ Publication No. 11(12)-0059, revised August 2012; web page content last reviewed April 2023. Used here for: the interview prompts (Figure 9); the list of sources (patient, family, bottles, community pharmacy, prescriber offices, past records, the patient's own list) with the caution that labels may not match actual use; Table 3 (one-to-one match, intended discrepancy, unintended discrepancy needing physician follow-up; formulary substitution and procedure holds listed as intended examples); the unintended discrepancy categories (omission, commission, different dose, route or frequency, different medication); and the three-source rule for external transfers (pre-hospital list, outside administration, own orders).

## 6. CMS discharge planning: what goes to the next provider

- **42 C.F.R. 482.43(b)** (hospitals): the hospital must discharge, and transfer or refer where applicable, the patient with all necessary medical information about the current course of illness and treatment, post-discharge goals of care and treatment preferences, at the time of discharge, to the post-acute providers, suppliers, facilities, agencies and outpatient practitioners responsible for follow-up or ancillary care.
- **Interpretive guidance, State Operations Manual Appendix A, tag A-0810** (Rev. 238, issued March 20, 2026): the necessary information "may include, but is not limited to" a medication list reconciled to identify changes made during the hospitalization, including prescription, OTC and herbal products, with the note that an actual list must be included, not just a referral to an electronic list elsewhere in the record; a list of allergies (food and drug) and drug interactions; pending tests; and for transfers, the advance directive and contact information for staff who know the patient.
- **42 C.F.R. 482.23(c)(6)(ii)** and tag A-0413: a hospital may let a patient self-administer medications brought from home only under a practitioner's order and hospital policy that identify and inspect the medications, secure them, and document each reported dose. Relevant when the patient's own supply is in use.
- **42 C.F.R. 485.642(b)**: the same transmission duty for critical access hospitals.
- **42 C.F.R. 483.21(c)(2)(iii)**: a long-term care facility's discharge summary must include reconciliation of all pre-discharge medications with post-discharge medications, prescribed and OTC.

The CMS list is guidance on content ("may include"); the regulation requires the necessary information to go at the time of discharge. Facility policy defines the format.

## 7. Hierarchy of authority and of evidence

Requirements: the CoP and its interpretive guidance for the provider type, then the accreditation standard for the organization's program, then facility policy (which sets who the qualified individual is and the timing), then WHO High 5s and MATCH as practice guidance.

Evidence of what the patient takes or received: administration records (outside MAR, inpatient MAR) and a prescriber's dated note govern over summaries, transfer forms, order-set defaults and auto-generated lists; a confirmed interview with label check governs over fill history alone. Name every conflict; the prescriber resolves it.

## 8. Points that are not set by any standard read

- No standard read sets a minimum number of BPMH sources; "more than one source" is WHO and MATCH guidance.
- No standard read defines review priority tiers; the tiers in the rules file are the skill's convention.
- Who may take a BPMH (pharmacist, technician, nurse) depends on facility policy and state practice rules `[VERIFY]`.
- FDA pregnancy letter categories (A, B, C, D, X) were removed from prescription labeling by the Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule (effective June 30, 2015, with phased conversion); describe pregnancy concerns from current labeling, not a letter.
